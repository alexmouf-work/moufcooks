# Coq au vin

Chicken thighs braised in red wine with smoky lardons, glazed shallots and golden mushrooms. The sauce is reduced and
glossy, with a cognac base. The skin finishes in the oven above the sauce, so it browns instead of going soft. The
lardons, shallots and mushrooms are each cooked separately and added at the end, so they stay distinct pieces rather
than melting into the sauce.

- **Serves:** 4 (see *For a crowd* for 6–8)
- **Same day:** about 3½ hours, 1½ of them hands-on; salt the chicken in the morning if you can · **Elevated:**
  cooked a day ahead and rested overnight
- **Status:** written 9 November 2026 from Alexander's starting recipe; first cook planned for 10 November, served at 7 pm.

## Shopping list

**The chicken**
- 8 bone-in, skin-on chicken thighs (about 1.4 kg), or 4 whole legs cut into thighs and drumsticks
- Fine sea salt, 1% of the chicken's weight (about 14 g)

**The garnish**
- 200 g smoked lardons, or a piece of smoked streaky bacon or pancetta cut into thick 1 cm batons
- 12 small shallots, or 16 pearl onions
- 300 g chestnut mushrooms, small ones if you can find them
- 40 g butter
- 1 tsp sugar

**The braise**
- 1 onion, 1 carrot and 1 celery stick
- 4 garlic cloves
- 1 tbsp tomato purée
- 50 ml cognac or brandy
- 1 bottle (750 ml) red Burgundy or another Pinot Noir; any light, fruity, not-oaky red
- 300 ml good chicken stock (fresh, not cubes)
- 4 sprigs thyme, 2 bay leaves, 1 small sprig rosemary and a few parsley stalks, tied together
- 1 tbsp olive oil
- 1 tsp redcurrant jelly (optional; see step 12)

**To finish**
- 20 g soft butter and 20 g plain flour, mashed together into a paste (*beurre manié*)
- A small bunch of flat-leaf parsley
- Black pepper

**To serve:** mashed potato (1 kg floury potatoes such as Maris Piper, 100 g butter, 150 ml milk or cream), or crusty bread.

**Kit:** a casserole dish or Dutch oven with a lid · a frying pan · a slotted spoon · a probe thermometer.

*Salting:* the longer the better, up to overnight, which is why it goes on in the morning. If you only manage an hour,
that still works; sear either straight after salting or after the full hour, never in between (see the ragù for why).
Less time means the meat is seasoned less deeply and the skin browns less readily.

**Elevated** (a day ahead): salt the chicken two nights before, and cook the whole braise the day before (steps 1–8).
Cool it in the liquid and refrigerate overnight. On the day, lift off the set fat, reheat it in the oven at 150 °C fan
for 40 minutes with the lid off so the skin re-crisps, then finish (steps 9–13).
*Why it's better:* a night's rest settles the sauce and melds the wine, cognac and smoke into one, and the meat takes
back liquid as it cools. It's also the calmest version to serve: on the day, you only glaze, fry and reduce.

## For a crowd (6–8 people, mixed breast and thigh)

A casserole won't hold a dozen pieces skin-up in one layer, so build the sauce in the casserole on the hob, then braise
in a large, deep roasting tray in the oven.

**Change the shopping list to:**
- **Chicken:** 8 bone-in, skin-on thighs and 5 bone-in, skin-on breasts ("supremes"). Bone-in breasts dry out far less
  than boneless ones.
- **Salt:** still 1% of the total weight of the chicken.
- **Garnish:** about 1½ times the main recipe. That's 300 g lardons, 18–20 shallots and 450 g mushrooms.
- **Braise:** also about 1½ times. That's two bottles of wine (you'll use about 1.1 litres), 450 ml stock, 75 ml cognac,
  and 1½ times the onion, carrot, celery and garlic.
- **Beurre manié:** 30 g butter and 30 g flour.
- **Mash:** 2 kg potatoes.
- **Kit:** add a roasting tray at least 5 cm deep that holds all 13 pieces in one layer, and foil.

**How it changes**
- **Brown the skin only** (step 3), in batches. Use the frying pan alongside the casserole to halve the time. Six to
  eight minutes skin-side down is enough; the meat cooks in the oven.
- **Make the sauce in the casserole** exactly as steps 4–7, but without the chicken.
- **Thighs into the tray first,** skin-up. Pour the sauce around them, to about two-thirds of the way up the meat, with
  the skin clear. Cover the tray tightly with foil. Oven at 150 °C fan (170 °C conventional) for 50 minutes.
- **Then take off the foil and add the breasts,** skin-up, tucked between the thighs. Cook uncovered for 30–35 minutes
  more.
- **They're done when** the breasts read **68 °C** at the thickest point and the thighs **80–85 °C**. Lift the breasts
  out first if they get there sooner.
- **Finish the sauce:** pour it from the tray back into the casserole, then reduce and thicken it as in step 12 while
  the chicken rests, uncovered, on warm platters.

*Why breasts go in later:* breast meat is lean, and juiciest just under 70 °C; past that it dries and turns stringy.
Thighs are full of connective tissue that needs time at a higher temperature to soften. Same tray, different clocks.
*Why foil, then none:* the foil keeps the thighs steaming gently at first. Uncovered, the skin crisps and the sauce
starts to reduce.

**Serving a mixed crowd:** slice the breasts thickly off the bone and lay them on one side of a platter, with the thighs on
the other, so people can choose. Spoon the sauce, shallots and mushrooms around them, then scatter over the lardons and
parsley.

## Method

### Salting (in the morning, or the night before)

**1. Salt the chicken.** Pat the thighs dry and sprinkle the salt evenly over both sides, most of it on the skin. Leave
them skin-up and uncovered on a rack or plate in the fridge.
*Why:* given hours, salt seasons the meat right through and helps it hold its juices. The cold air dries the skin, so
it browns hard tomorrow instead of steaming.
*Why not a wine marinade:* it's the traditional method, but it leaves the chicken wet, so it won't brown, and the
wine's acid can turn the outside of the meat mealy. The wine depth comes from reducing it in step 6 instead.

### Building the braise

**2. Render the lardons.** Put them in the cold casserole with a dash of water (2–3 tbsp) over a medium-low heat. Let the
water cook off and the fat run, then let them crisp. Lift them out with the slotted spoon and leave the fat in the pan.
*Why:* the water melts the fat out evenly before the meat colours, as with the pancetta in the ragù. Their smoky fat
becomes the cooking fat for everything after.

**3. Brown the chicken, skin-side first.** Add the olive oil to the lardon fat, over a medium-high heat. Pat the thighs
dry again and lay them in skin-side down, in two batches, with space between them. Leave them alone for 6–8 minutes until
the skin is deep golden and releases from the pan by itself, then turn them for 2 minutes. Lift them onto a plate.
*Why:* the browned skin gives the sauce its roasted depth, and it's what crisps again in the oven later. Moving the
pieces too early tears the skin and leaves it stuck to the pan.

**4. Soften the vegetables.** Pour off all but 2 tbsp of fat. Turn the heat to medium, add the onion, carrot and celery,
diced small, with a pinch of salt, and cook for 8–10 minutes until soft and lightly golden. Add the garlic for the last
minute, then the tomato purée, and fry for 2–3 minutes until it darkens to brick red.
*Why:* the vegetables give the sauce body and sweetness, where the original recipe relied on the wine alone. Frying the
purée caramelises it and takes away its raw, tinny taste.

**5. Cognac.** Take the pan off the heat and pour in the cognac. Scrape the bottom hard while it bubbles.
- **To flambé it** (optional, and fun): stand back, with nothing above the pan, and light the cognac with a long match.
  Let the flames die down by themselves, which takes about 20 seconds.
- **Otherwise:** just let it bubble for 30 seconds.

*Why:* the cognac lifts the browned crust off the pan and adds a warm, rounded sweetness underneath the wine. Flaming
burns off the harsh alcohol quickly. Taking the pan off the heat first stops the vapour catching unexpectedly.

**6. Add the whole bottle of wine and boil it down by a third,** about 10–15 minutes over a high heat.
*Why:* this boils off the raw alcohol and concentrates the wine's fruit, so the finished sauce tastes deeply of wine
rather than sharp and boozy.

**7. Assemble the braise.** Add the stock, the herb bundle and half the lardons. Nestle the chicken in **skin-side up**,
so the liquid comes about two-thirds of the way up the meat and the skin stays clear of it. Bring it back to a simmer.
*Why skin-up and above the liquid:* skin under liquid goes flabby. Above it, the skin keeps its colour and crisps again
once the lid comes off. Half the lardons flavour the sauce; the rest are saved for crunch at the end.

**8. Oven at 150 °C fan (170 °C conventional): 50 minutes with the lid on, then 25 minutes with it off.** The thighs are
done when they reach 80–85 °C at the thickest point and the meat gives easily when pressed.
*Why:* the oven gives steady, even heat, and leaves the hob free for the garnish. Thighs are at their most tender well
above the temperature that makes them safe, as the connective tissue softens. Lid off at the end lets the skin crisp and
the sauce start to reduce.

### The garnish, while it braises

**9. Glaze the shallots.** If you're using pearl onions and haven't peeled them, pour boiling water over them for a
minute, then drain; the skins slip off. In the frying pan, cook the whole shallots in 20 g butter with the sugar and a
pinch of salt, over a medium heat, for 3–4 minutes. Add 100 ml water, cover and simmer for 10 minutes until tender.
Uncover, and let the liquid boil off until they're coated in a shiny glaze and turn golden. Tip them into a bowl.
*Why:* glazed this way they're sweet, glossy and tender through, and they stay whole: a sweet bite against the wine.

**10. Fry the mushrooms.** Wipe out the pan. Halve or quarter the mushrooms so the pieces are about the same size. Fry them
in the remaining 20 g butter over a high heat, in one layer, without stirring for the first 3 minutes, then until golden
all over, about 8 minutes in all. Season with salt and pepper only at the end.
*Why:* a crowded, salted pan makes mushrooms release water and stew. Hot, uncrowded and salted last, they brown and stay
meaty.

### Finishing

**11. Lift out the chicken and herb bundle.** Put the chicken on a warm plate, skin-up, and keep it uncovered so the skin
stays crisp. Skim off most of the fat from the sauce with a ladle.

**12. Reduce and thicken the sauce.** Boil it on the hob until it's reduced by about a third. Then whisk in the beurre
manié a small piece at a time, letting each piece melt and the sauce thicken before adding more. Stop when it coats the
back of a spoon and looks glossy. Taste it:
- **Season** with black pepper, and salt only if it needs it.
- **If it tastes sharp,** whisk in the teaspoon of redcurrant jelly.

*Why:* the butter-and-flour paste thickens the sauce without lumps, and the butter gives it a shine. Adding it bit by
bit lets you stop at exactly the thickness you want. Redcurrant jelly is an old French trick: a little sweetness rounds
off the wine's acidity.

**13. Bring it together.** Stir the glazed shallots and the mushrooms into the sauce for a minute to warm through. Return
the chicken skin-up. Scatter the rest of the lardons and the chopped parsley over the top.

## Serving

Spoon a bed of mash onto warm plates. Put two thighs on it, then spoon the sauce, shallots and mushrooms around the
chicken rather than over it, so the skin stays crisp. Finish with the reserved lardons and parsley.

**Mash:** boil peeled, quartered floury potatoes in salted water for about 20 minutes until completely soft. Drain, let
them steam dry for 2 minutes, then mash or rice them with the butter and the warm milk or cream. Season well.

Crusty bread is good too, for the sauce.

## Leftovers

- **Keeps** 3 days in the fridge, and it's better on day two. Reheat it in a 150 °C fan oven, lid off, so the skin
  re-crisps.
- **What to make:**
  - **Pasta:** pull the meat off the bone into the sauce and toss it through tagliatelle.
  - **A pie:** chicken, sauce and garnish under a puff-pastry lid, 25 minutes at 200 °C fan.
  - **Loaded fries or a baked potato:** shredded chicken and sauce, crisp lardons and parsley.
  - **Toast:** chicken and mushrooms in sauce on thick toast, with a fried egg on top.

## Notes

- **Wine:** Burgundy is the classic, because it's Pinot Noir: light, fruity, and it doesn't turn bitter when reduced.
  Avoid heavy, oaky reds, which go harsh. Cook with a wine you'd drink, and serve the same kind with dinner.
- **No probe thermometer to hand?** The thighs are ready when the meat pulls away from the bone easily.
