# Beef cheek ragù

A rich, slow-braised ragù of beef cheek, red wine and tomato, with roasted bone marrow folded through at the end. The
cheeks cook until they fall apart at a touch.

- **Serves:** 6–8 with pasta (1.25 kg raw cheek gives roughly 750 g cooked meat)
- **Standard:** about 6½–7 hours from start to table. This is the version that was cooked and loved.
- **Elevated:** over three days, with overnight salting and an overnight rest. Deeper and more settled in flavour.
- **Status:** cooked (standard) 17 October 2026. Verdict: "very, very pleased". See the cook's notes at the bottom.

## Shopping list

**The meat**
- 1.25 kg beef cheeks, trimmed (about 3 cheeks)
- 12 g fine sea salt (1% of the meat's weight; about 2 level teaspoons)
- About 1 kg **marrow bones, cut into 5–7 cm rounds** or split lengthways. Ask the butcher to cut them (see *Bones*).
- 1 tbsp neutral oil, if the marrow gives little fat

**The base**
- 150 g pancetta, finely diced
- 20 g butter
- 2 onions (about 300 g), 2 carrots (about 200 g) and 2 celery sticks (about 150 g)
- 5 garlic cloves
- 3 tbsp tomato purée (double concentrate)

**The braise**
- 500 ml dry red wine: medium-bodied and not oaky, such as Chianti, Montepulciano d'Abruzzo, Barbera or Côtes du Rhône
- 2 × 400 g tins whole plum tomatoes (San Marzano if you can find them)
- 400–600 ml beef stock, homemade or good shop-bought fresh stock (not cubes)
- 2 bay leaves, 4 sprigs thyme and 1 small sprig rosemary, tied together
- 6–8 whole black peppercorns (optional)
- 1 Parmesan rind

**To finish**
- 30 g cold butter
- 1 tsp red wine vinegar or a lemon
- Black pepper

**To serve:** tagliatelle and Parmesan (see Serving).

**Kit:** a casserole dish or Dutch oven of 5 litres or more, with a lid · a roasting tin · baking paper · a ladle · two
forks. An oven thermometer helps, because fan ovens often run hot.

## Bones

Marrow is the flavour you want to come through clearly, and only **marrow bones** give it: thick leg bones **cut
through** so the marrow shows. Bones this hard can't be cut at home with kitchen knives, so ask the butcher to saw them
into rounds or "canoes" (split lengthways). It takes them a minute.

**If all you have are whole, thin bones:** roast them whole for about 20 minutes. They give little solid marrow, but
plenty of liquid fat, and that fat *is* the marrow flavour. Pour it off and keep it: use it for searing, and save about 2
tbsp in the fridge to stir in at the finish in place of the scooped marrow. Put any bones that fit into the braise.

## Versions

**Standard** (about 6½–7 hours)

| From start | Do |
|---|---|
| 0:00 | Oven on to 200 °C fan. Halve and salt the cheeks (step 1). |
| 0:05 | Marrow bones into the oven (step 2). Dice the vegetables. |
| 0:25 | Bones out; scoop the marrow; oven down to 130 °C fan. Start the pancetta and vegetables (step 3). |
| 0:50 | Vegetables out of the pan. |
| 1:00 | Sear the cheeks (step 4). |
| 1:15 | Vegetables back; tomato purée; wine (steps 5–6). |
| 1:30 | Assemble the braise (step 7). |
| 1:40 | Into the oven (step 8). |
| 4:40 | Turn the cheeks; lid off (step 9). |
| 5:10–6:10 | Tender? Out to rest for 30 minutes (steps 10–11). Put the pasta water on. |
| 5:40–6:40 | Finish and serve (steps 12–15). |

**Elevated** (three days)

| When | Do |
|---|---|
| Day 1, evening (10 min) | Trim the cheeks and salt them **whole**; leave uncovered in the fridge overnight (step 1). Optional: make bone stock during the day (see the notes). |
| Day 2 (about 6½ hours) | Cook as for the standard version, from the bones onwards. Whole cheeks take a little longer, about 4½–5 hours in the oven. Cool in the liquid, then refrigerate overnight (step 11). |
| Day 3 (40 min) | Lift off the set fat, reheat gently, finish and serve (steps 12–15). |

*Why elevated tastes deeper:* overnight, the salt seasons the cheeks right to the centre and dries their surface so they
brown harder. After cooking, a night in the fridge lets the sauce settle and the flavours meld into one, and the meat
reabsorbs liquid as it cools. The fat sets into a lid that lifts off in one piece.

## Method

### Salt and bones

**1. Salt the cheeks.** Check for any shiny, silvery membrane left on the outside and slice it off. Sprinkle the 12 g of
salt evenly over every side.
- **Standard:** halve each cheek first, so the halves are about equally thick. Leave them uncovered for **one hour**,
  at room temperature or in the fridge.
- **Elevated:** keep them whole. Leave them uncovered on a rack in the fridge overnight.

*Why halves for the standard version:* six pieces have far more surface to brown than three, and that browning becomes flavour in
the sauce. They also soften a little sooner. Don't go smaller than halves: small chunks dry out and turn stringy before
the collagen melts.
*Why the hour:* in the first 20–40 minutes, salt pulls moisture to the surface, which then steams instead of browning.
By an hour most of it has been drawn back in, carrying the seasoning with it. So sear either straight after salting or
after the full hour, never in between.

**2. Roast the marrow bones, cut side up, at 200 °C fan (220 °C conventional) for about 20 minutes,** until the marrow is
soft and just starting to bubble. Pour off the rendered fat and keep it. Scoop out **about half the marrow** with a
teaspoon, then cover it and refrigerate. The rest stays in the bones. Turn the oven down to **130 °C fan (150 °C
conventional)**.
*Why:* roasting gives the bones a deep, nutty flavour that raw bones don't have. Marrow is used twice: the half left in
the bones melts into the sauce over the long cook and gives depth, and the reserved half goes in at the very end so it
tastes clearly of marrow. Much more than 20 minutes and the marrow melts away into the tin.

### One pan: vegetables, searing, the braise

**3. Render the pancetta, then soften the vegetables.** Dice the onions, carrots and celery to 5 mm, and slice the
garlic. Put the pancetta in the casserole over a medium-low heat with **a dash of water** (2–3 tbsp). Let the water cook
off and the fat run, then let the pancetta crisp. Add the butter, onion, carrot, celery and a pinch of salt, and cook
gently for 15–20 minutes, stirring now and then, until soft, sweet and lightly golden. Add the garlic for the last 2
minutes. Then tip everything out into a bowl.
*Why the water:* it keeps the pan cool at first, so the fat melts out evenly before the meat starts to colour. That
gives more fat to cook in, and the pancetta crisps all the way through instead of burning at the edges.
*Why slowly:* this base (a *soffritto*) is the backbone of any ragù. Slow cooking turns the onion and carrot sweet; fast
cooking makes them bitter. Dicing them small lets them melt into the sauce.

**4. Sear the cheeks in the same pan.** Lift out any loose bits of vegetable left behind, but keep the thin brown film
stuck to the bottom. Add 1–2 tbsp of the marrow fat (or oil) and let the pan get properly hot, so a cheek sizzles loudly
the moment it goes in. Pat the cheeks dry and brown them for 3–4 minutes a side without moving them, until deep chestnut
brown all over. Leave a finger's width between pieces, and do two batches if they don't fit. Lift them out onto a plate.
*Why one pan:* the browned film from the vegetables and the crust from the searing all stay in the same pot and end up
in the sauce. Loose bits of vegetable would burn at searing heat, which is why they come out first.
*Why sear:* browning creates savoury, roasted flavours that the braise can't make by itself. Crowded meat steams and
stays grey.

**5. Vegetables back in, then fry the tomato purée.** Return the vegetables and pancetta. Push them aside, add the purée to
the bare patch, and fry for 3–4 minutes, stirring, until it darkens from bright red to brick red and starts to catch.
*Why after the searing:* the purée is the ingredient most likely to burn. Fried before the searing, any left on the pan
would scorch at searing heat and turn bitter. Fried now, it also picks up the crust from the cheeks.
*Why fry it at all:* frying caramelises the purée's sugars and takes away its raw, tinny taste. This is where much of the
deep tomato flavour comes from.

**6. Add the wine and boil it down by half** (8–10 minutes) over a high heat, scraping the bottom.
*Why:* this boils off the harsh alcohol and concentrates the wine's fruit. Wine that isn't reduced tastes sharp and
boozy, even after hours in the oven. The scraping lifts every browned bit into the sauce.

**7. Assemble the braise.** Crush the whole tomatoes into the casserole by hand and swirl a splash of water round each
tin to get the rest out. Return the cheeks and their juices. Tuck the marrow bones in around them, with the herbs, the
peppercorns and the Parmesan rind. Pour in stock until the liquid comes **about three-quarters of the way up the meat**,
not over it. Bring it to a gentle simmer.
*Why whole tins:* whole tinned tomatoes are usually better quality than chopped ones. Chopped tomatoes are often treated
to keep their shape, so they never fully break down into the sauce.
*Why three-quarters:* this is a braise, not a boil. The covered pot fills with steam and cooks the tops of the cheeks,
and less liquid means a more concentrated sauce. Bringing it to a simmer first means the oven doesn't spend its first
hour just heating the pot.
*Why no salt here:* the cheeks, pancetta, stock and rind are all salty, and the sauce reduces by about a third. Taste
and salt at the end, never now.

### The oven

**8. Cook covered for 3 hours.** Press a sheet of baking paper onto the surface, put the lid on fully, and cook at 130 °C
fan.
*Why:* at this low setting the liquid barely trembles, at about 90–95 °C inside the pot. That's hot enough to melt the
collagen into gelatine over a few hours, but gentle enough that the muscle fibres don't clench and squeeze out their
juice. The baking paper and full lid stop the fan drying the exposed meat and slow evaporation.
Check it at 1 hour. If there isn't a single bubble, raise the oven by 10 °C. If it's bubbling hard, lower it by 10 °C.
Short of time? Use 140 °C fan from the start, but no higher: above that the liquid boils and the meat tightens.

**9. After 3 hours, turn the cheeks and take off the lid and paper.** Top up with stock or water if the liquid has
dropped below halfway. Cook for another 30–90 minutes (up to 2 hours for whole cheeks).
*Why:* with the lid off, the sauce reduces and the exposed meat browns a little, which deepens the flavour again.

**10. Test for doneness from 3½ hours** (4½ for whole cheeks). Press a piece with the back of a spoon. It should collapse
with no resistance, and a fork should twist through it easily. If it doesn't, give it another 30 minutes and test again.
*Why:* undercooked cheek is the most common way this dish goes wrong: it's chewy, not tender. Overcooking within 6 hours
is unlikely, so when in doubt, give it longer.

**11. Rest it.**
- **Standard:** rest for 30 minutes out of the oven. Tilt the pot so the fat pools on one side, and lift most of it off
  with a ladle, keeping a spoonful in for flavour.
- **Elevated:** let it cool in its liquid, then refrigerate overnight. Next day, lift off the set fat in one piece
  (keep a spoonful), and reheat the pot gently on the hob, covered, for 20–30 minutes until bubbling at the edges.

*Why:* resting lets the meat take back some of the liquid so it stays moist, and lets the fat rise so it's easy to take
off.

### Finishing

**12. Take out the cheeks, bones, herbs and rind.** Scrape any marrow still in the bones into the sauce.

**13. Reduce the sauce.** Simmer it on the hob for 10–20 minutes until it coats a spoon thickly and looks glossy. Cook
the pasta meanwhile.
*Why:* a ragù should cling to whatever it's served with, not pool around it.

**14. Shred the cheeks.** Pull them into bite-sized chunks with two forks, leaving some pieces bigger than others, and
stir them back into the sauce. For risotto, leave them in larger pieces (see Serving).
*Why uneven:* the mix of tender shreds and bigger chunks gives the sauce texture, so no two forkfuls are the same.

**15. Finish it off the heat.** Stir in the reserved marrow (or saved marrow fat) and the cold butter until they melt
and the sauce turns glossy. Season with black pepper, then taste for salt. Add the vinegar or lemon a few drops at a
time, until the sauce tastes bright rather than heavy.
*Why:* this is where the marrow comes through clearly. Added at the end it tastes of marrow, where the half cooked for
hours has become general richness. The acid wakes up flavours that hours of cooking have dulled. Seasoning last avoids
over-salting, because reducing concentrates the salt already there.

## Serving

**Pasta: tagliatelle.** Pappardelle was tried on the first cook and was too wide to wrap round the meat nicely, even
cooked exactly right. Tagliatelle is narrower but still flat enough to carry the shredded meat. Use 100 g dried or 120 g
fresh pasta per person. Cook it 1–2 minutes short of the packet time, then toss it in a wide pan with the ragù and a
splash of the pasta water for a minute or two, until the sauce clings and shines. Serve with Parmesan.

**Plating:** twist the pasta into a nest in a warm, shallow bowl, spoon over a little extra sauce, and top with two or
three bigger chunks of cheek kept back while shredding. Shave Parmesan over with a peeler, add a pinch of gremolata and a
crack of pepper, then wipe the rim.

**Risotto: risotto alla milanese.** This is the classic partner for braised beef in Milan (it's what ossobuco is served
on), and it's traditionally made with beef marrow, so it echoes the ragù. Best with the elevated version, when the ragù
only needs reheating and your hands are free to stir. Serve the cheeks in larger pieces on top, with gremolata. For 4:
- 320 g Carnaroli rice
- 1 small onion, very finely chopped
- 30 g bone marrow (scoop an extra bone) or butter
- 100 ml dry white wine
- about 1.2 litres of hot stock
- a generous pinch of saffron (about 0.2 g), steeped in a ladle of the stock
- 40 g cold butter
- 60 g Parmesan, grated

Soften the onion in the marrow, toast the rice for 2 minutes, then add the wine. Add the stock a ladle at a time,
stirring, for 16–18 minutes, adding the saffron halfway. Off the heat, beat in the cold butter and Parmesan
(*mantecatura*) and rest it for 2 minutes.

**Gremolata** (good with either, essential with the risotto): the zest of 1 lemon, a small bunch of flat-leaf parsley
and 1 small garlic clove, all chopped finely together. Scatter it over just before serving. It cuts through the richness.

## Leftovers

- **Keeps** 4 days in the fridge, or 3 months frozen. Freeze it before the final butter and marrow, and add those when
  reheating.
- **Proven so far:** more pasta, loaded fries, and over rice. All three were excellent.
- **Also worth trying:** stuffed into a baked potato with cheese; as a pie filling under mash; in a toasted sandwich with
  melted cheese.

## Notes

- **Bone stock (elevated, if you have spare bones).** Cover roasted non-marrow bones with cold water, add vegetable
  trimmings and a bay leaf, and simmer gently with the lid ajar for 4–6 hours. Strain it and reduce it to about 600 ml.
- **Red or white wine?** Red, for this: it gives depth, colour and the richness you want with beef. White makes a lighter,
  more tomato-led sauce. Cook with a wine you'd happily drink, and serve the rest of the bottle with dinner.
- **No anchovies** in this version, by choice. The Parmesan rind gives the savoury depth instead.
- **Fan ovens vary.** The temperatures here assume a true 130 °C fan. Trust the bubbles in step 8 more than the dial.
