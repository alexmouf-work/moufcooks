# Beef cheek ragù

A rich, slow-braised ragù of beef cheek, red wine and tomato, with roasted bone marrow folded through at the end. The
cheeks cook until they fall apart at a touch.

- **Serves:** 6–8 with pasta (1.25 kg raw cheek gives roughly 750 g cooked meat)
- **Time:** overnight salting · 1 h 15 min prep · 4–5 h in the oven · 30 min to finish
- **Best made a day ahead.** It tastes better the next day, and the fat is easy to lift off once cold.
- **Status:** written 17 October 2026; not yet cooked. Add notes from the first cook at the bottom.

## Ingredients

**The meat**
- 1.25 kg beef cheeks
- 12 g fine sea salt (1% of the meat's weight; about 2 level teaspoons)
- Marrow bones, about 1 kg, cut into 5–7 cm rounds or split lengthways (see *Bones* below)
- Any other beef bones you have (optional, for stock)
- 1 tbsp neutral oil, if the marrow gives little fat

**The base**
- 150 g pancetta, finely diced (optional, adds a savoury, porky depth)
- 20 g butter
- 2 onions (about 300 g), 2 carrots (about 200 g) and 2 celery sticks (about 150 g), all diced to 5 mm
- 5 garlic cloves, finely sliced
- 3 tbsp tomato purée (double concentrate)
- 3 anchovy fillets in oil (optional, melt away to a savoury depth; nobody will taste fish)

**The braise**
- 500 ml dry red wine: medium-bodied and not oaky, such as Chianti, Montepulciano d'Abruzzo, Barbera or Côtes du Rhône
- 2 × 400 g tins whole plum tomatoes (San Marzano if you can find them)
- 400–600 ml beef stock, homemade or good shop-bought fresh stock (not cubes)
- 2 bay leaves, 4 sprigs thyme and 1 small sprig rosemary, tied together
- 1 Parmesan rind (optional)

**To finish**
- The reserved marrow
- 30 g cold butter
- 1 tsp red wine vinegar or a squeeze of lemon
- Black pepper; more salt to taste

**Kit:** a casserole dish or Dutch oven of 5 litres or more, with a lid · a roasting tin · baking paper · two forks.
An oven thermometer helps, because fan ovens often run hot.

## Bones

Marrow is the flavour you want to come through clearly, and only **marrow bones** give it: leg bones cut through so the
marrow shows. Knuckles, ribs and neck bones give gelatine (body) but little marrow. Check what you have. If it isn't
marrow bones, ask a butcher for about 1 kg of marrow bones cut into rounds or "canoes" (split lengthways). They're cheap.

Other bones can go into a stock the day before (see the notes), or into the pot if there's room.

## Method

### The day before

**1. Trim the cheeks.** Cut off the tough silvery membrane on the outside and any large lumps of hard fat. Leave the
cheeks whole.
*Why:* that membrane never softens; it stays rubbery however long it cooks. The connective tissue running *through* the
cheek is different: it melts into gelatine, and that's what makes cheek so silky. Whole cheeks also stay juicier and
shred into better pieces at the end.

**2. Salt them and leave them uncovered in the fridge overnight** (12–24 h), on a rack or plate. Sprinkle the 12 g of salt
evenly over every side.
*Why:* given hours, salt travels into the middle of the meat, so it's seasoned all the way through rather than just on the
surface. The fridge air also dries the outside, which then browns instead of steaming when you sear it.

### The cooking day

**3. Take the cheeks out of the fridge an hour before searing.** Heat the oven to 200 °C fan (220 °C conventional).
*Why:* cold meat cools the pan and browns unevenly.

**4. Roast the marrow bones, cut side up, for about 20 minutes,** until the marrow is soft and just starting to bubble.
Roast any other bones in the same tin for 40–45 minutes, until deep brown. Pour off the rendered fat and keep it.
Scoop out **about half the marrow** with a teaspoon, then cover it and refrigerate. The rest stays in the bones.
*Why:* roasting gives the bones a deep, nutty flavour that raw bones don't have. Marrow is used twice: the half left in
the bones melts into the sauce over the long cook and gives depth. The reserved half goes in at the very end, so its
flavour stays distinct instead of fading into the background. More than about 20 minutes and the marrow melts away into
the tin.

Turn the oven down to **130 °C fan (150 °C conventional)** when the bones come out.

**5. Sear the cheeks.** Heat 1–2 tbsp of the marrow fat (or oil) in the casserole over a medium-high heat. Pat the cheeks
dry, then brown them in two batches for 3–4 minutes a side, without moving them, until a deep chestnut brown all over.
Set them aside on a plate and keep the juices.
*Why:* browning creates savoury, roasted flavours that the braise can't make by itself. Searing in batches keeps the pan
hot; crowded meat steams and stays grey. The brown crust left on the pan is flavour too, and it comes up in step 8.

**6. Soften the vegetables.** Turn the heat to medium-low. If you're using pancetta, cook it for 5 minutes until its fat
runs. Add the butter, onion, carrot, celery and a pinch of salt. Cook gently for 15–20 minutes, stirring now and then,
until soft, sweet and lightly golden. Add the garlic for the last 2 minutes.
*Why:* this base (a *soffritto*) is the backbone of any ragù. Slow cooking turns the onion and carrot sweet. Fast cooking
makes them bitter. Dicing them small lets them melt into the sauce, so you get no chunks of carrot in the finished ragù.
The water they release also loosens the crust from the pan.

**7. Fry the tomato purée and anchovies.** Push the vegetables aside, add the tomato purée and anchovies to the bare
patch, and fry for 3–4 minutes, stirring, until the purée darkens from bright red to brick red and starts to catch.
*Why:* frying caramelises the purée's sugars and takes away its raw, tinny taste. This is where much of the deep tomato
flavour comes from. The anchovies dissolve and add savouriness, as Parmesan does.

**8. Add the wine and boil it down by half** (8–10 minutes) over a high heat, scraping the bottom of the pan.
*Why:* this boils off the harsh alcohol and concentrates the wine's fruit. Wine that isn't reduced tastes sharp and
boozy, even after hours in the oven. The scraping lifts the browned bits from searing into the sauce.

**9. Add the tomatoes.** Crush the whole tomatoes into the pot by hand and swirl a splash of water round each tin to get
the rest out.
*Why whole tins:* whole tinned tomatoes are usually better quality than chopped ones. Chopped tomatoes are often
treated to keep their shape, so they never fully break down into the sauce.

**10. Assemble the braise.** Return the cheeks and their juices. Tuck the marrow bones in around them, along with the
herbs and the Parmesan rind. Pour in stock until the liquid comes **about three-quarters of the way up the meat**, not
over it. Bring it to a gentle simmer on the hob.
*Why:* this is a braise, not a boil. The covered pot fills with steam and cooks the tops of the cheeks, and less liquid
means a more concentrated sauce at the end. Bringing it to a simmer first means the oven doesn't spend the first hour just
heating the pot.

**11. Into the oven, covered, for 3 hours.** Press a sheet of baking paper onto the surface, put the lid on, and cook at
130 °C fan.
*Why:* at this low setting the liquid barely trembles, at about 90–95 °C inside the pot. That's hot enough to melt
collagen into gelatine over a few hours, but gentle enough that the muscle fibres don't clench and squeeze out their
juice. The baking paper stops the fan oven drying the exposed meat and slows evaporation.
Check it at 1 hour. If there isn't a single bubble, raise the oven by 10 °C. If it's bubbling hard, lower it by 10 °C.

**12. After 3 hours, check the liquid level and turn the cheeks over.** Top up with stock or water if the liquid has dropped below
halfway. Then return the pot to the oven **without the lid or paper** for another 1–2 hours.
*Why:* with the lid off, the sauce reduces and the exposed meat browns a little, which deepens the flavour again.

**13. Test for doneness.** Press a cheek with the back of a spoon. It should collapse with no resistance, and a fork should twist
through it easily. If it doesn't, give it another 30 minutes and test again. Expect about 4–5 hours in total.
*Why:* undercooked cheek is the most common way this dish goes wrong: it's chewy, not tender. Within your 4–6 hour
window there's little risk of overcooking it, so when in doubt, give it longer.

**14. Cool it in its liquid.** Ideally, refrigerate it overnight. If you're eating the same day, rest it for 30 minutes.
*Why:* as it cools, the meat reabsorbs some of the liquid and stays moist. Overnight, the flavours settle together and the
fat sets on top, where it's easy to lift off.

### Finishing (on the day you eat it)

**15. Lift off most of the fat, and take out the cheeks, bones and herbs.** Scrape any marrow still in the bones into the
sauce. Keep a spoonful of fat in the sauce for flavour.

**16. Reduce the sauce.** Simmer it on the hob for 10–20 minutes until it coats a spoon thickly and looks glossy.
*Why:* a ragù should cling to whatever it's served with, not pool around it.

**17. Shred the cheeks.** Pull them into bite-sized chunks with two forks and stir them back into the sauce. For risotto,
leave them in larger pieces (see Serving).

**18. Finish it off the heat.** Stir in the reserved marrow and the cold butter until they melt and the sauce turns
glossy. Season with black pepper, then taste for salt. Add the vinegar or lemon a few drops at a time, until the sauce
tastes bright rather than heavy.
*Why:* this is where the marrow flavour comes through clearly. Added at the end, it tastes of marrow, where the half
cooked for hours has turned into general richness. The acid wakes up flavours that hours of cooking have dulled.
Season last because the stock, anchovy, Parmesan and reducing all add salt.

## Serving

**Pasta (my pick for a first cook): pappardelle.** Its wide ribbons hold shredded meat better than any other shape. Use
100 g dried or 120 g fresh pasta per person. Cook it 1–2 minutes short of the packet time, then toss it in a wide pan with the
ragù and a splash of the pasta water for a minute or two, until the sauce clings and shines. Serve with Parmesan.
It's the simpler choice for a first cook: the pasta takes ten minutes, and the ragù is the star.

**Risotto: risotto alla milanese.** This is the classic partner for braised beef in Milan (it's what ossobuco is served
on), and it's traditionally made with beef marrow, so it echoes the ragù. Serve the cheeks in larger pieces on top rather
than shredded through, with gremolata. For 4:
- 320 g Carnaroli rice
- 1 small onion, very finely chopped
- 30 g bone marrow (scoop an extra bone) or butter
- 100 ml dry white wine
- about 1.2 litres of hot stock
- a generous pinch of saffron (about 0.2 g), steeped in a ladle of the stock
- 40 g cold butter
- 60 g Parmesan, grated

Soften the onion in the marrow, toast the rice for 2 minutes, then add the wine. Add the stock a ladle at a time, stirring,
for 16–18 minutes, adding the saffron halfway. Off the heat, beat in the cold butter and Parmesan (*mantecatura*) and rest it
for 2 minutes. The catch: risotto needs stirring right at the end, at the same time as you're finishing the ragù.

**Gremolata** (good with either, essential with the risotto): the zest of 1 lemon, a small bunch of flat-leaf parsley and
1 small garlic clove, all chopped finely together. Scatter it over just before serving. It cuts through the richness.

## Notes

- **Red or white wine?** Red, for this: it gives depth, colour and the richness you want with beef. White makes a lighter,
  more tomato-led sauce (some Bolognese recipes use it). Cook with a wine you'd happily drink, and serve the rest of the bottle
  with dinner.
- **Bone stock (optional, the day before).** Cover the roasted non-marrow bones with cold water, add vegetable trimmings and
  a bay leaf, and simmer gently with the lid ajar for 4–6 hours. Strain it, then reduce it to about 600 ml. It's richer and
  more gelatinous than shop-bought, and it uses the bones you already have.
- **Keeps** 4 days in the fridge, or 3 months frozen. Freeze it before the final butter and marrow, and add those when
  reheating.
- **Fan ovens vary.** The temperatures here assume a true 130 °C fan. Trust the bubbles in step 11 more than the dial.
