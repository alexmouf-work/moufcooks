# Beef cheek ragù

A rich, slow-braised ragù of beef cheek, red wine and tomato, with roasted bone marrow folded through at the end. The
cheeks cook until they fall apart at a touch. This version is made in a single day.

- **Serves:** 6–8 with pasta (1.25 kg raw cheek gives roughly 750 g cooked meat)
- **Time:** about 6½ hours from start to table: 1 h 20 min hands-on, 3½–4½ h in the oven, 1 h to rest and finish
- **Status:** written 17 October 2026; first cook the same day. Add notes at the bottom.

## Ingredients

**The meat**
- 1.25 kg beef cheeks, trimmed (about 3 cheeks)
- 12 g fine sea salt (1% of the meat's weight; about 2 level teaspoons)
- Marrow bones, about 1 kg, cut into 5–7 cm rounds or split lengthways (see *Bones* below)
- 1 tbsp neutral oil, if the marrow gives little fat

**The base**
- 150 g pancetta, finely diced (optional, adds a savoury, porky depth)
- 20 g butter
- 2 onions (about 300 g), 2 carrots (about 200 g) and 2 celery sticks (about 150 g), all diced to 5 mm
- 5 garlic cloves, finely sliced
- 3 tbsp tomato purée (double concentrate)

**The braise**
- 500 ml dry red wine: medium-bodied and not oaky, such as Chianti, Montepulciano d'Abruzzo, Barbera or Côtes du Rhône
- 2 × 400 g tins whole plum tomatoes (San Marzano if you can find them)
- 400–600 ml beef stock, homemade or good shop-bought fresh stock (not cubes)
- 2 bay leaves, 4 sprigs thyme and 1 small sprig rosemary, tied together
- 1 Parmesan rind (optional, but worth it for savoury depth)

**To finish**
- The reserved marrow
- 30 g cold butter
- 1 tsp red wine vinegar or a squeeze of lemon
- Black pepper; more salt to taste

**Kit:** a casserole dish or Dutch oven of 5 litres or more, with a lid · a large frying pan · a roasting tin · baking
paper · a ladle · two forks. An oven thermometer helps, because fan ovens often run hot.

## Bones

Marrow is the flavour you want to come through clearly, and only **marrow bones** give it: leg bones cut through so the
marrow shows. Knuckles, ribs and neck bones give gelatine (body) but little marrow. If yours aren't marrow bones, ask a
butcher for about 1 kg of marrow bones cut into rounds or "canoes" (split lengthways). They're cheap. Other bones can go
into the pot if there's room.

## Method

### The salting hour

**1. Halve the cheeks and salt them.** Cut each cheek in two so the halves are about equally thick. Check for any shiny,
silvery membrane left on the outside and slice it off. Sprinkle the 12 g of salt evenly over every side, then leave the
pieces uncovered on a plate for **one hour**, at room temperature or in the fridge.
*Why halves:* six pieces have far more surface to brown than three, and that browning becomes flavour in the sauce.
They also sear more evenly and soften a little sooner. Don't go smaller than halves: small chunks dry out and turn
stringy before the collagen melts. Cut pieces lose a little more juice than whole ones, but shredded into a rich sauce
you won't notice.
*Why an hour of salt:* in the first 20–40 minutes, salt pulls moisture to the surface, which then steams instead of
browning. By an hour most of it has been drawn back in, carrying the seasoning with it. So sear either straight after
salting or after the full hour, never in between. (Overnight salting is better still; see the notes.) An hour at room
temperature is safe, and it takes the chill off the meat, which helps it brown evenly.

**2. Roast the marrow bones, cut side up, at 200 °C fan (220 °C conventional) for about 20 minutes,** until the marrow is
soft and just starting to bubble. Pour off the rendered fat and keep it for searing. Scoop out **about half the marrow**
with a teaspoon and set it aside. The rest stays in the bones. Turn the oven down to **130 °C fan (150 °C conventional)**.
*Why:* roasting gives the bones a deep, nutty flavour that raw bones don't have. Marrow is used twice: the half left in
the bones melts into the sauce over the long cook and gives depth, and the reserved half goes in at the very end so it
tastes clearly of marrow. Much more than 20 minutes and the marrow melts away into the tin.

**3. Soften the vegetables in the casserole** while the cheeks finish salting. Over a medium-low heat, cook the pancetta,
if using, for 5 minutes until its fat runs. Add the butter, onion, carrot, celery and a pinch of salt. Cook gently for
15–20 minutes, stirring now and then, until soft, sweet and lightly golden. Add the garlic for the last 2 minutes.
*Why:* this base (a *soffritto*) is the backbone of any ragù. Slow cooking turns the onion and carrot sweet; fast cooking
makes them bitter. Dicing them small lets them melt into the sauce. Cooking them now, during the salting hour, rather
than after the searing, saves about half an hour.

**4. Fry the tomato purée.** Push the vegetables aside, add the purée to the bare patch, and fry for 3–4 minutes,
stirring, until it darkens from bright red to brick red and starts to catch.
*Why:* frying caramelises the purée's sugars and takes away its raw, tinny taste. This is where much of the deep tomato
flavour comes from.

### Searing and the braise

**5. Pour the wine into the casserole and boil it down by half** (8–10 minutes) over a high heat, scraping the bottom.
*Why:* this boils off the harsh alcohol and concentrates the wine's fruit. Wine that isn't reduced tastes sharp and
boozy, even after hours in the oven.

**6. Meanwhile, sear the cheeks in the frying pan.** Heat 1–2 tbsp of the marrow fat (or oil) over a medium-high heat.
Pat the pieces dry and brown them for 3–4 minutes a side without moving them, until deep chestnut brown all over. Leave
a finger's width between pieces; if they don't all fit, do two batches.
*Why:* browning creates savoury, roasted flavours that the braise can't make by itself. Crowded meat steams and stays
grey. Searing in a separate pan lets it happen while the wine reduces.

**7. Deglaze the frying pan.** Take it off the heat, pour in a splash of the stock, and scrape up the brown crust. Tip it
all into the casserole along with the cheeks and their juices.
*Why:* that crust is concentrated flavour; it shouldn't be left in the pan.

**8. Assemble the braise.** Crush the whole tomatoes into the casserole by hand and swirl a splash of water round each
tin to get the rest out. Tuck the marrow bones in around the cheeks, with the herbs and the Parmesan rind. Pour in stock
until the liquid comes **about three-quarters of the way up the meat**, not over it. Bring it to a gentle simmer.
*Why whole tins:* whole tinned tomatoes are usually better quality than chopped ones. Chopped tomatoes are often treated
to keep their shape, so they never fully break down into the sauce.
*Why three-quarters:* this is a braise, not a boil. The covered pot fills with steam and cooks the tops of the cheeks,
and less liquid means a more concentrated sauce. Bringing it to a simmer first means the oven doesn't spend its first
hour just heating the pot.

### The oven

**9. Cook covered for 3 hours.** Press a sheet of baking paper onto the surface, put the lid on, and cook at 130 °C fan.
*Why:* at this low setting the liquid barely trembles, at about 90–95 °C inside the pot. That's hot enough to melt the
collagen into gelatine over a few hours, but gentle enough that the muscle fibres don't clench and squeeze out their
juice. The baking paper stops the fan drying the exposed meat and slows evaporation.
Check it at 1 hour. If there isn't a single bubble, raise the oven by 10 °C. If it's bubbling hard, lower it by 10 °C.
Short of time? Use 140 °C fan from the start, but no higher: above that the liquid boils and the meat tightens.

**10. After 3 hours, turn the cheeks and take off the lid and paper.** Top up with stock or water if the liquid has
dropped below halfway. Cook for another 30–90 minutes.
*Why:* with the lid off, the sauce reduces and the exposed meat browns a little, which deepens the flavour again.

**11. Test for doneness from 3½ hours.** Press a piece with the back of a spoon. It should collapse with no resistance,
and a fork should twist through it easily. If it doesn't, give it another 30 minutes and test again.
*Why:* undercooked cheek is the most common way this dish goes wrong: it's chewy, not tender. Overcooking within 5 hours
is unlikely, so when in doubt, give it longer.

**12. Rest it for 30 minutes out of the oven, then spoon off the fat.** Tilt the pot so the fat pools on one side and lift
it off with a ladle. Keep a spoonful in for flavour.
*Why:* resting lets the meat take back some of the liquid so it stays moist, and lets the fat rise so it's easy to take
off.

### Finishing

**13. Take out the cheeks, bones and herbs.** Scrape any marrow still in the bones into the sauce.

**14. Reduce the sauce.** Simmer it on the hob for 10–20 minutes until it coats a spoon thickly and looks glossy. Cook
the pasta meanwhile.
*Why:* a ragù should cling to whatever it's served with, not pool around it.

**15. Shred the cheeks.** Pull them into bite-sized chunks with two forks and stir them back into the sauce. For risotto,
leave them in larger pieces (see Serving).

**16. Finish it off the heat.** Stir in the reserved marrow and the cold butter until they melt and the sauce turns
glossy. Season with black pepper, then taste for salt. Add the vinegar or lemon a few drops at a time, until the sauce
tastes bright rather than heavy.
*Why:* this is where the marrow comes through clearly. Added at the end it tastes of marrow, where the half cooked for
hours has become general richness. The acid wakes up flavours that hours of cooking have dulled. Season last because the
stock, Parmesan and reducing all add salt.

## Serving

**Pasta (my pick): pappardelle.** Its wide ribbons hold shredded meat better than any other shape. Use 100 g dried or
120 g fresh pasta per person. Cook it 1–2 minutes short of the packet time, then toss it in a wide pan with the ragù and a
splash of the pasta water for a minute or two, until the sauce clings and shines. Serve with Parmesan.

**Risotto: risotto alla milanese.** This is the classic partner for braised beef in Milan (it's what ossobuco is served
on), and it's traditionally made with beef marrow, so it echoes the ragù. Serve the cheeks in larger pieces on top rather
than shredded through, with gremolata. For 4:
- 320 g Carnaroli rice
- 1 small onion, very finely chopped
- 30 g bone marrow (scoop an extra bone) or butter
- 100 ml dry white wine
- about 1.2 litres of hot stock
- a generous pinch of saffron (about 0.2 g), steeped in a ladle of the stock
- 40 g cold butter
- 60 g Parmesan, grated

Soften the onion in the marrow, toast the rice for 2 minutes, then add the wine. Add the stock a ladle at a time,
stirring, for 16–18 minutes, adding the saffron halfway. Off the heat, beat in the cold butter and Parmesan
(*mantecatura*) and rest it for 2 minutes. The catch: risotto needs stirring right at the end, at the same time as you're
finishing the ragù.

**Gremolata** (good with either, essential with the risotto): the zest of 1 lemon, a small bunch of flat-leaf parsley
and 1 small garlic clove, all chopped finely together. Scatter it over just before serving. It cuts through the richness.

## Notes

- **With more time:** salt the whole cheeks overnight, uncovered in the fridge, so they're seasoned right through and
  brown better. Then cook a day ahead, cool in the liquid and refrigerate overnight: the flavour settles together and the
  fat sets on top. Whole cheeks are fine when cooked this way.
- **Red or white wine?** Red, for this: it gives depth, colour and the richness you want with beef. White makes a lighter,
  more tomato-led sauce. Cook with a wine you'd happily drink, and serve the rest of the bottle with dinner.
- **Bone stock (if you have spare bones and a day).** Cover roasted non-marrow bones with cold water, add vegetable
  trimmings and a bay leaf, and simmer gently with the lid ajar for 4–6 hours. Strain it and reduce it to about 600 ml.
- **Keeps** 4 days in the fridge, or 3 months frozen. Freeze it before the final butter and marrow, and add those when
  reheating.
- **Fan ovens vary.** The temperatures here assume a true 130 °C fan. Trust the bubbles in step 9 more than the dial.
